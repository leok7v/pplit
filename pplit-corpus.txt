A company might initiate a price drop on a product for several strategic reasons, including:

1. **Increase Demand:** Lowering the price of a product can stimulate demand, especially if the product is price sensitive. A lower price can attract new customers who were previously discouraged by the cost.

2. **Market Penetration:** A company may reduce prices to enter a new market or gain a larger share in an existing market. The aim is to attract customers from competitors by offering more value for a lower price.

3. **Clear Inventory:** If a company has excess inventory, it may lower prices to clear out stock. This is common for products that are perishable, seasonal, or about to be replaced by newer models.

4. **Response to Competition:** If competitors lower their prices or introduce cheaper alternatives, a company might reduce its prices to remain competitive and retain its market share.

5. **Economic Conditions:** During economic downturns, consumers become more price-sensitive. Lowering prices can help maintain sales volume when consumers are looking to cut spending.

6. **Increase Market Visibility:** Introducing a product at a lower price can generate buzz and attract attention, increasing brand visibility and potentially leading to higher sales volumes.

7. **Promotional Strategy:** Price drops can be part of promotional campaigns to increase short-term sales or introduce potential customers to a product or a brand.

8. **Cost Reductions:** If a company manages to reduce its production or operational costs, it may pass these savings onto consumers in the form of lower prices, thus potentially increasing volume sales without affecting profit margins.

9. **Product Life Cycle Management:** As a product matures and moves towards the end of its life cycle, companies might reduce its price to extract value from the remaining market before discontinuing the product.

10. **Encourage Bulk Purchases:** Lowering the price per unit when bought in larger quantities can encourage customers to buy more, increasing overall sales even if the profit margin per unit is reduced.

Understanding and strategically managing product pricing is crucial for sustaining profitability and market presence. Each price adjustment decision is context-specific and must consider market conditions, company objectives, and consumer behavior.

Yes, it is quite usual to use a UUID (Universally Unique Identifier) as a primary key in various database systems. There are several reasons why developers opt for UUIDs over traditional auto-incrementing integers. Some of the reasons include:

1. **Uniqueness Across Systems**: UUIDs can be generated in a distributed system without needing communication between the parts to ensure uniqueness. This makes them ideal for modern, distributed applications including microservices and cloud-native applications, where different services might generate new entities independently.

2. **Scalability**: Since UUIDs can be generated anywhere without risk of collision, they enable easier scaling of applications. This is particularly important for systems that intend to grow significantly or need to incorporate data from various sources seamlessly.

3. **Security and Privacy**: Sequential IDs (such as those that are auto-incremented) can sometimes leak information about the data, such as how many records exist or the rate at which records are being created. Using UUIDs can help obscure such details, adding a layer of privacy and security.

4. **Flexibility**: If a system is designed to merge datasets or integrate with other systems, using UUIDs from the start can make this process smoother, as there's no need to resolve ID conflicts.

However, using UUIDs also comes with drawbacks:

- **Storage Size**: UUIDs typically occupy more space than integer-based keys—16 bytes for a UUID compared to 4 bytes for a typical integer. Over millions of records, this can add up.
  
- **Performance Issues**: Due to their size and non-sequential nature, UUIDs can lead to performance issues, particularly in indexing, joins, and sorting operations. Most modern databases can handle UUIDs efficiently, but care must be taken in their implementation to avoid potential issues.
  
- **Readability and Usability**: For human operators, UUIDs are less readable and harder to use when manually querying the database, compared to simple integer IDs.

Despite these potential downsides, the unique advantages of UUIDs—especially their global uniqueness and suitability for distributed systems—make them a popular choice for many applications. Careful consideration of the specific needs of the application and its potential growth can help in deciding whether UUIDs are the right choice for a primary key.
